Section 2.1

What is a state governed by the rule of law?

Rules exist everywhere people live together; they set out rights (what you're entitled to) and obligations (what you must do). Some are unwritten (letting people off the train first), others written down. This chapter focuses on legal standards — rules of conduct the government has laid down in statutory laws and regulations. All of these together are called "the law", and they keep society orderly (cycling on the same side, stopping at red).

Rule of law
A legal system in which fundamental rights protect individuals from abuses of power and arbitrary violations. You can no longer be arrested or imprisoned just because a mayor or minister feels like it.

Origins

For centuries kings did as they pleased and people had few rights. Resistance grew in the 18th century (the Enlightenment): citizens gained knowledge and became critical. This led to the French Revolution (1789) and the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, establishing rights like liberty, property and safety — and the rule of law was born.

Core tasks of the government

Law enforcement
The state must ensure that we obey the law. To do so it has more power than citizens and the sole right to use force — the monopoly on violence (e.g. police using pepper spray, or in extreme cases shooting a suspect).
Legal protection
Laws protect us from abuses of power by the government itself. As individuals we're vulnerable against a powerful state, so e.g. the Netherlands has a complete ban on torture — even when lives are at stake.
Case in point: the torture scenario at the start of the section. Even to save lives, the police may not torture the terrorist — legal protection and the torture ban still apply.

Countries without the rule of law

In an authoritarian state one person or a small group decides the rules, eliminating opponents without trial and denying rights. Seen in dictatorships (Saudi Arabia, North Korea, Turkmenistan); countries like Turkey, Russia and Poland don't always respect fundamental freedoms either.

Section 2.2

Conditions for a state governed by the rule of law

Independent judges are a prerequisite (they don't let bias guide their work and ensure a fair trial), but more is needed. A state governed by the rule of law must meet at least three conditions:

1 · Constitutional rights — fundamental rights are laid down in the constitution.
2 · Separation of powers — the trias politica has been established.
3 · Principle of legality — the government may only act on a legal basis.

1 · Constitutional rights

Constitution
The most important document in the country: it defines the fundamental rights of inhabitants and how the state is organised.
Fundamental rights (= human rights)
The basic rights you need to live a life of human dignity. Defined in constitutions and in treaties like the UDHR. Two categories:
  • Classic fundamental rights — e.g. equal treatment, freedom of religion/expression, bodily integrity. The government must guarantee these; you can go to court to enforce them.
  • Fundamental social rights — e.g. work, health care, housing. The government can't guarantee these but must make an effort (an unemployed person can't sue for a job).
Amendments: NL has had a constitution since 1798. Voting extended to all men in 1917 and women in 1919; social rights added in 1983. Amending it needs two rounds of voting with elections in between (first a simple majority, then a two-thirds majority).

2 · Separation of powers (trias politica)

Montesquieu: "every man invested with power is apt to abuse it." The trias politica means no single person or body has absolute power, to prevent abuse.

BranchTask in the Netherlands
Legislativemakes the laws (Parliament + government; a minister usually presents a bill, Parliament approves)
Executiveimplements approved laws (the government; ministers direct their civil servants)
Judicialrules on conflicts and whether someone broke the law (judges)
Checks and balances
The three branches keep each other in check; each is accountable to the other two (e.g. Parliament questions a minister; a court can call the minister to order if a bill conflicts with EU rules).

The judicial branch up close

Independent judges ensure fair and impartial justice and protect citizens from the government (e.g. the Urgenda case forced the state to cut CO₂ by 25%). They also prevent vigilante justice (eigenrichting) by guaranteeing fair trials. Independence is supported by their lifetime appointment (can't be fired over unpopular rulings) and public hearings. If a judge seems biased you can seek disqualification via a challenge (wraking).

Section 2.3

Crime and investigation

Police and judicial authorities must follow the rules, because investigations can infringe our rights. This section is about criminal law: investigating and prosecuting criminal offences.

Areas of law

TypeGoverns
Public lawthe relationship between citizens and public authorities — includes criminal law (what behaviour is punishable)
Private / civil lawhow private entities interact — includes legal entities (rechtspersonen), marriage, divorce, inheritance, renting

Legal standards work best when aligned with our sense of justice. Laws evolve with our views (same-sex marriage in NL in 2001; the #MeToo debate redefined non-consensual sex as rape).

Crime, major & minor offences

Crime
All the unlawful acts specified in the law. Most are listed in the Criminal Code.
Major offences
Serious crimes (theft, assault, murder, DUI, hacking). Max penalty: life in prison. You get a criminal record.
Minor offences
Less serious (cycling without lights, public urination, vandalism). Max sentence: one year.

Investigating crimes

1 · Police collect information — evidence, witnesses, arrests, all written in a police report. The public prosecutor leads and monitors fairness.
2 · The public prosecutor then has three options: dismiss, resolve it directly, or take it to court. A private citizen cannot start criminal proceedings; all prosecutors work for the Public Prosecution Service (they don't work for the police).

Investigative powers of the police

The police can't randomly detain or frisk you — only with reasonable suspicion that you're breaking the law (then you're a suspect). These are coercive measures. When apprehended you must show ID; on arrest you go to the station for questioning. Heavier measures need permission from a magistrate (rechter-commissaris) or the prosecutor:

  • Entering/searching a house — needs reasonable suspicion; a search also needs a warrant.
  • Wiretap — monitoring phone calls and internet traffic.
  • Preventive frisking — searching without prior suspicion, only in security-risk areas (stadiums, airports). Body searches always need permission.
  • Holding a suspect beyond nine hours — extendable by three days, max twice.
  • Infiltration — an undercover agent may not incite others; solicitation, entrapment and incitement are forbidden.

The public prosecutor's three options

OptionWhat it means
Dismissalno further prosecution (insufficient evidence, minor offence, or already punished enough — e.g. the "1000th pickpocket" shamed in the media)
Out-of-court settlement / penalty ordera fine or community service without going to court. A penalty order (strafbeschikking) also establishes guilt; object in writing to still get a court hearing
Prosecutionsending the case to court; the suspect becomes the defendant. Until proven guilty the presumption of innocence applies
Section 2.4

The justice system: courts and sentencing

The prosecutor acts "in the name of the community"; victims can't prosecute themselves. Complex or serious cases go before a full-bench panel (meervoudige strafkamer) of three judges. The court session is the hearing, usually public (so anyone can check it's fair) — but closed if the suspect is a minor.

Procedure & appeal

Every suspect has the right to a lawyer right after arrest (assigned pro bono if you can't afford one). Before the hearing you get a subpoena (dagvaarding) stating the charges. The hearing runs in set steps:

1 Opening — judge checks details, states rights.
2 Charges — prosecutor reads out the offences.
3 Examination — judge questions the defendant; exhibits and witnesses.
4 Motion for verdict — prosecutor argues guilt and asks for a specific punishment (the demands).
5 Defence — the lawyer argues for the accused.
6 Closing statement — defendant can apologise or stress innocence.
7 Judgement — judge decides guilty or not and the punishment.

If the convicted person or prosecutor disagrees, they can appeal to the court of appeal (hoger gerechtshof), and after that to the Supreme Court (Hoge Raad) — which only reviews whether the law was applied correctly, not the facts.

Why do we punish? (goals)

  • Vengeance and retribution — crime shouldn't be rewarded ("an eye for an eye").
  • Deterrence — discourage the offender and others from (re)offending.
  • Prevention of vigilante justice — punishment is for the courts, not private citizens.
  • Resocialisation — re-education so offenders conform to society (e.g. anti-aggression training).
  • Public safety — long sentences keep repeat offenders from committing new crimes.

Judges must follow the Criminal Code and can't invent punishments; corporal punishment and the death penalty are banned (Constitution Article 114).

Types of punishment (principal penalties)

Principal penaltyDetails
Custodial sentenceprison; max is life (life-sentenced prisoners can petition for release after 25 years)
Community sentencecommunity service, or behavioural training for juveniles (not for adults)
Financial penaltyfines up to €1 million; substitute detention = 1 day for every €50 unpaid

An additional penalty can be added (revoking a licence, stadium ban). A sentence can be conditional (probation): comply and you don't serve that part. Beyond the sentence a judge can impose a criminal justice measure (e.g. compensation, or TBS).

TBS (terbeschikkingstelling)
Compulsory detention under hospital order, imposed if the offender was mentally disturbed and not fully accountable ("non compos mentis"). Treatment includes supervised leave to practise living outside the clinic; >99% of those given leave don't escape and re-offend less than prison inmates.

Juvenile justice

  • Under 12: can be questioned but not brought to court (not criminally responsible); youth care may step in.
  • 12–17: juvenile criminal law. Minor offences → community service via Halt. Juvenile detention: max 1 year (12–15) or 2 years (16–17), with education and reintegration.
  • 16–23: adolescent criminal law — the judge may apply juvenile or adult law, since people mature at different paces.

Self-test

Tap a question to reveal the answer. Try to answer out loud first.

Define the rule of law.

A legal system in which fundamental rights protect individuals from abuses of power and arbitrary violations.

What are the government's two core tasks?

Law enforcement (making sure we obey the law) and legal protection (protecting us from abuses of power by the government).

What is the state monopoly on violence?

The state's sole right to use force (e.g. police using pepper spray), needed to enforce the law.

Name the three conditions for a state governed by the rule of law.

Fundamental rights in the constitution, separation of powers (trias politica), and the principle of legality.

Difference between classic and social fundamental rights?

Classic rights (e.g. free speech) must be guaranteed and are enforceable in court; social rights (e.g. housing) the government must only make an effort to provide.

What is the trias politica and who devised it?

The separation of powers into legislative, executive and judicial branches, so no one has absolute power — devised by Montesquieu.

What does each of the three branches do?

Legislative makes laws, executive implements them, judicial rules on conflicts and law-breaking.

What is a "challenge" (wraking)?

A motion to disqualify a judge who appears biased, so a different judge takes the case.

Difference between public law and private law?

Public law governs citizen–government relations (incl. criminal law); private/civil law governs how private entities interact (marriage, contracts, etc.).

Major vs minor offences — one difference?

Major offences are serious crimes (max life in prison, criminal record); minor offences are less serious (max one year).

When can police frisk you?

Only with reasonable suspicion that you're breaking the law (or preventive frisking in security-risk areas like stadiums/airports).

What are the public prosecutor's three options after an investigation?

Dismiss the case, settle out of court / issue a penalty order, or prosecute (take it to court).

What does the presumption of innocence mean?

You are innocent until the court has established proof that you are guilty.

Name three steps of the hearing.

Any of: opening, charges, examination, motion for verdict, defence, closing statement, judgement.

What is the route of appeal?

Court of appeal (hoger gerechtshof), then the Supreme Court (Hoge Raad), which only checks whether the law was applied correctly.

Name three goals of punishment.

Any of: retribution, deterrence, preventing vigilante justice, resocialisation, public safety.

Name the three principal penalties.

Custodial sentence, community sentence, financial penalty.

What is TBS?

Compulsory detention under hospital order for offenders who were mentally disturbed and not fully accountable for their actions.

Which criminal law applies to 12–17-year-olds, and to 16–23-year-olds?

12–17: juvenile criminal law. 16–23: adolescent criminal law (judge may choose juvenile or adult law).
