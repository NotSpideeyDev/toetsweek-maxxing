Section 4.1

What is a pluralist society?

Pluralist society
A society which allows differences in lifestyle, religion and other cultural features. People are free to live by their own norms and values.
Culture
All the values, norms, customs and other cultural features shared by people within a group or society. Norms and values are key cultural features.
Dominant culture
All the values, norms, customs and cultural features shared by a majority of the population. In NL: speaking Dutch, equality between men and women, celebrating King's Day.
Tolerance
Acceptance that people are different from you and behave or think differently (e.g. gay marriage, tolerating soft drugs). Not automatically guaranteed — protests and attacks show this.
Subculture
When some values, norms, customs and cultural features within a group differ from the dominant culture (students, conservative Christians, skaters). Everyone belongs to several at once.
Cultural diversity
A wide range of different subcultures and lifestyles around you — the result of living in a pluralist society.

Subculture features can move into the dominant culture (tattoos, once a subculture, now normal) or disappear from it (smoking was generally accepted until the late 1990s).

The six factors behind cultural diversity

FactorWhat it means
Living environmentCity vs. village/countryside — different levels of anonymity and community.
GenerationDifferences between you and (grand)parents — a different frame of reference: the sum of your knowledge, experiences, norms, values and habits.
Position in societyEducation, profession and income shape lifestyle (manners, tastes, hobbies).
GenderThe cultural differences between men and women, linked to role-based patterns: general expectations about how a person should behave.
ReligionEvery faith has its own views, customs and holidays.
Migration backgroundCreates ethnic subcultures: people feel connected by a shared country of origin and its values, norms and customs.
Note on terms: the old words autochtoon/allochtoon took on negative associations and caused division, so the official terms are now "people with a Dutch background" and "people with a migration background".

Evolving culture: some features never change (the ban on murder or theft), but most develop over time, across locations and between groups. Culture is dynamic.

Section 4.2

Becoming who you are

Nature vs. nurture debate
"Nature" = inherited traits shape who you are (temperament, sexual preference). "Nurture" = behaviour is learned from your environment and culture. Current view: a complex interaction of both.
Socialisation
The process through which people learn the values, norms and other cultural features of the group and society they belong to. Starts at birth; also called cultural transfer.
Socialising institutions
The groups and organisations that transfer specific values, norms and customs: family, school, friends, faith community, sports club, government and the media.
Internalisation
When people take on values, norms and customs and automatically behave the way those around them expect (cycling on the right, queuing in shops).

How socialisation takes place

  • Imitation — common in children; they copy the people around them.
  • Information — people give instructions, tips and explanations.
  • Social control — the ways people encourage or force others to follow current norms (a reprimand for belching, praise for finishing homework).

Identity

Personal identity
How a person sees themselves — hobbies, taste in music, dress, and personality (patient/impatient, calm/hyper).
Social identity
The part of your identity derived from the groups and cultures you feel connected to. You belong to many groups, so you have multiple social identities.

Can you choose who you want to be?

How much freedom you have to shape your own identity depends on the culture:

TypeDescriptionExamples
IndividualisticEmphasis on personal development and individual freedom; loose ties; everyone looks after themselves.the Netherlands

Section 4.3

Pigeon-holing people

People have an innate need to belong. The downside: we tend to exclude — or even discriminate against — people outside our group. Central question: how does pigeon-holing (hokjesdenken) work and what are its consequences?

Categorisation
Your brain creates "pigeon-holes" (categories) and automatically assigns people, objects or situations to them. It serves a useful purpose — you instantly know a chair is for sitting.
Stereotype
An exaggerated, predefined impression of a group of people (e.g. "girls are good at languages, boys at maths").
Prejudice
Forming an opinion about someone without knowing them personally — based solely on the category you assign to them.
Discrimination
Unequal treatment of people based on a feature that is not relevant to the situation (gender, skin colour, ethnic origin, age, religion, sexual orientation, disability).

The chain: categorisation → stereotypes → prejudice → discrimination. Socialisation shapes our stereotypes (family, friends, and especially the media). Discrimination creates and maintains social inequality, and those who face it are more likely to have mental-health problems.

In the Netherlands discrimination occurs on the job market, in education, in criminal investigations, and on the street and social media.

From "us vs. them" to division

Racism
Originally: sorting people into races and assuming one is superior (there are no biological races). More commonly: hurtful, humiliating statements about — or discrimination against — people based on skin colour or ethnic origin.
Othering (us-them thinking)
Defining an "us" group and a "them" group. People view their own group positively and others negatively, which strengthens the internal bond.
Polarisation
Us-them thinking taken to extremes: perceived differences keep growing and groups drift apart. Can lead to violent conflict, even civil war (e.g. the Zwarte Piet debate).
Social cohesion
The mutual connection that makes people feel they belong and willing to help each other. Strengthened by a shared language, values, norms and national holidays. Discrimination and polarisation harm it.
Exam link: discrimination and polarisation both divide society into groups, which is exactly why they damage social cohesion — that connection is the counter-force.

Self-test

Tap a question to reveal the answer. Try to answer out loud first.

Define a pluralist society.

A society that allows differences in lifestyle, religion and other cultural features.

What is the difference between dominant culture and a subculture?

Dominant culture = shared by the majority. Subculture = a group whose values/norms/customs differ from that dominant culture (but it's still part of it).

Define tolerance and give an example.

Acceptance that people are different and behave/think differently — e.g. gay marriage or tolerating soft drugs.

Name the six factors behind cultural diversity.

Living environment, generation, position in society, gender, religion, migration background.

What is a frame of reference?

The sum total of your knowledge, experiences, norms, values and habits.

Explain the nature vs. nurture debate.

Nature = inherited traits shape you; nurture = behaviour is learned from environment/culture. Current view: a complex interaction of both.

Define socialisation and name three socialising institutions.

The process of learning the values, norms and cultural features of your group/society. Institutions: e.g. family, school, the media (also friends, faith community, sports club, government).

What are the three ways socialisation takes place?

Imitation, information and social control.

What is internalisation?

When people take on values/norms/customs and automatically behave the way those around them expect.

Difference between personal identity and social identity?

Personal = how you see yourself (hobbies, taste, personality). Social = the part of identity derived from the groups/cultures you feel connected to.

Contrast individualistic and collectivist cultures.

Individualistic = emphasis on personal freedom, loose ties (NL). Collectivist = group/family interests come first, close ties (parts of Africa, Middle East).

Contrast masculine and feminine cultures.

Masculine = clearly separate gender roles, men dominant (Morocco, Mexico). Feminine = roles overlap (NL, Sweden).

What is categorisation and why is it useful?

The brain assigning people/objects/situations to predefined categories; it lets you understand the world quickly (a chair is for sitting).

Difference between a stereotype and prejudice?

Stereotype = exaggerated, predefined impression of a group. Prejudice = an opinion about someone without knowing them, based only on their category.

Define discrimination.

Unequal treatment based on a feature that is not relevant to the situation (gender, skin colour, age, religion, etc.).

What is othering, and how can it escalate?

Defining an "us" vs. "them" group with a positive self-view and negative view of others; taken to extremes it becomes polarisation, which can cause violent conflict.

Why do discrimination and polarisation harm social cohesion?

Because they divide society into opposed groups, weakening the mutual connection and sense of belonging that hold a society together.
