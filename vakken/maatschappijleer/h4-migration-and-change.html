Section 4.4

Migration to the Netherlands

Migration
Moving to a different country. A big step — you exchange familiar surroundings for an unfamiliar language, people and culture.

Three motives for migration

MotiveWhy people leave
EconomicThey cannot find work or are fleeing poverty.
PoliticalThe political situation — armed conflict, or persecution for faith, opinions or sexual orientation.
SocialTo join relatives abroad or be with a partner from another country.

Migration in the past (two big flows from 1945)

  • Former colonies: after the Dutch East Indies (Indonesia), Surinam and the Antilles gained independence, many residents moved to NL for a better future, work and study.
  • Guest workers (gastarbeiders): from the 1960s the economy needed low-skilled workers — first from Italy, Spain and Greece, later mainly Turkey and Morocco. Called "guest" workers because everyone assumed they would not stay. Many Southern Europeans returned; many Turkish and Moroccan workers stayed and brought their families.

Who is allowed to stay?

The Netherlands follows a restrictive immigration policy (strict requirements), within international treaties.

Refugees
People who leave their homes to flee war and violence. Most stay in their own region; some flee to the West and apply for asylum (a refuge offering protection and shelter). NL signed the UN Refugee Convention (1951). Status is hard to get: applications are thoroughly investigated, you need valid ID and must prove you are in danger.
Migrant workers (within the EU)
EU citizens travel freely (Schengen Agreement, 1985). Many come from Poland, Bulgaria, Romania for seasonal labour, horticulture, construction and transport — often accepting lower wages.
Knowledge migrants (outside the EU)
People from the USA, Japan, China, South Korea, India who bring in-demand knowledge (science, technology). They work for universities or companies like Shell, Samsung or Philips and usually get a residence permit.
Chain migration (volgmigratie)
Migration as a result of family reunification and family formation — a right in the European Convention on Human Rights (1950). NL adds conditions: a valid residence permit, earning ≥130% of minimum wage, being ≥21, and the partner passing the basic civil integration exam abroad.

Who is NOT allowed to stay?

  • Economic refugees: poverty is not grounds for a residence permit, so they must return.
  • Undocumented migrants: people who do not have legal permission to live and work in a country but stay anyway (tens of thousands in NL).
Source 12 in one line: knowledge migrants, chain migrants and recognised refugees → (temporary) residence permit. Asylum seekers without the right to asylum, those who exhausted all legal remedies, or who entered via human trafficking → no legal basis to stay → repatriation (return, forced or voluntary).

Policy debate: should the borders be more open or closed? It's a balance between caring for the Dutch population and caring for refugees — and how much people see immigration as a threat to national culture and identity.

Section 4.5

Integration is not automatic

Three ways for cultures to co-exist

FormWhat it means
AssimilationPeople give up their own cultural identity and change completely to adapt to the dominant culture. Not expected in NL; if it happens, it's voluntary.
SegregationGroups of people living separate lives, hardly crossing paths. Can harm migrants' chances in education/work and cause clashes — the government tries to prevent it.
IntegrationThe middle ground: combining cultural groups through mutual adaptation. Newcomers learn Dutch, respect core values and participate; in return society leaves room for their own customs and traditions.

An open society is the ideal the government encourages: lots of freedom to live by your own values and norms, reflected in the Constitution — freedom of religion (Art. 6), freedom of expression (Art. 7), freedom of education (Art. 23) and a ban on discrimination (Art. 1). You can be yourself, as long as you respect the dominant values and norms.

Tensions: three perspectives

Newcomers
People not born in NL who came to live here later — by choice (knowledge migrants) or necessity (refugees). They gain opportunities and safety but feel a sense of loss, must learn the language, and can clash with Dutch norms (e.g. gender equality). After five years they can become citizens — only with a passed civil integration exam and no criminal record.
Dutch people with a migration background
Many were born here. The first generation has strong ties to their origin; their children grow up with two cultures. Later generations feel more connected to NL. Some still don't feel they belong and face discrimination.
Dutch people without a migration background
Experience mixed feelings of loss and enrichment. Enrichment: new food, languages, music. Loss: feeling they no longer recognise their own neighbourhood. Tensions rise when Dutch values and norms feel under pressure (e.g. the Zwarte Piet debate).
Radicalisation
When the ideas and/or behaviour of a person or group become more extreme and increasingly clash with the norms and values of a democratic state governed by the rule of law. Radicalised extremists are more willing to consider violence. Risk is higher among young people who don't feel at home, face discrimination and lack future prospects, and where there's a strong "us vs. them" feeling. Applies to radical Islamic ideas and to nationalism and racism.
Exam tip (source 17): "Should those people wear normal clothes?" The correct reply is B — No, everyone is allowed to decide for themselves what to wear. That's the open-society / integration principle, not assimilation.
Section 4.6

The Netherlands is changing

Culture is dynamic. Until the second half of the 1900s the dominant culture's values were different — e.g. until 1956 married women were legally incompetent (handelingsonbekwaam): they couldn't open a bank account or sign a contract without their husband's permission. Obedience, duty and frugality were prized. Three big trends changed this:

1 · Individualisation

Pillarisation (verzuiling)
Organising society around faith or political persuasion — separate schools, TV channels and newspapers for Catholics, Protestants and socialists.
Individualisation
From the 1960s the church's role shrank and the pillars blurred; people saw themselves as free individuals. New values: individual freedom, personal development and personal responsibility.
  • Freedom & responsibility: more freedom to choose, but also more pressure — you're responsible for your own success (choice overload, performance stress).
  • Downside: looser ties → loneliness; a stronger focus on self → selfishness and antisocial behaviour (online hate speech, road aggression).

2 · Globalisation

Globalisation
The process through which people all over the world become more and more connected — driven by communication media, transport, air travel and the internet.
  • Enrichment: adopted customs not originally Dutch — burgers, shawarma, Black Friday, Valentine's Day, TikTok/Instagram/WhatsApp.
  • Threat: it can erode unique features (e.g. English replacing Dutch at universities), prompting a surge in nationalism and calls to protect local culture and national identity.

3 · Emancipation

Emancipation
People actively working to achieve equal rights and equal treatment, for groups historically disadvantaged by gender, sexuality or ethnic background.
  • Women's rights: the vote (1919), the 1960s liberation movement, economic independence, a more equal division of labour, and protected access to contraception and abortion. Still ongoing — women are under-represented in top jobs and paid less.
  • Gay rights: same-sex marriage legalised in 2001. The definition widened into the LGBTQIA+ movement (lesbian, gay, bisexual, trans, queer/questioning, intersex, asexual and more), now fighting for things like legal gender recognition for non-binary people. Discrimination and violence still occur.
  • Minority rights: ethnic minorities building a presence (media, politics); growing support for Black Lives Matter — but resistance leads to harsh, sometimes violent debates.
Where is NL heading? The SCP survey finds people are happy with their own lives but worried about how we co-exist: diminishing solidarity, less respect, growing division and polarisation. That sets up section 4.7.
Check yourself

Self-test

Tap a question to reveal the answer. Try to answer out loud first.

Name the three motives for migration.

Economic (no work / poverty), political (conflict / persecution), and social (joining family or a partner).

Who were the guest workers and why the name?

Low-skilled workers (first Italy/Spain/Greece, later Turkey/Morocco) invited from the 1960s. Called "guest" because everyone assumed they wouldn't stay permanently — but many did.

What is a restrictive immigration policy?

A policy that sets strict requirements for who is allowed to stay in the country.

What is the difference between a refugee and an economic refugee in terms of staying?

A refugee flees war/violence and can apply for asylum. An "economic refugee" flees poverty — which is not grounds for a residence permit, so they must return.

Define knowledge migrants and chain migration.

Knowledge migrants bring in-demand expertise (science/tech) from outside the EU. Chain migration = migration through family reunification and family formation.

Who are undocumented migrants?

People who don't have legal permission to live and work in a country but continue to stay there.

Contrast assimilation, segregation and integration.

Assimilation = give up your culture to fully adapt. Segregation = groups living separate lives. Integration = combining groups through mutual adaptation (the Dutch ideal).

Why does the government try to prevent segregation?

It harms migrants' chances in education and the job market and causes misunderstandings and clashes between groups.

What is an open society, and which Constitution articles support it?

A society with lots of freedom to live by your own values/norms. Supported by freedom of religion (Art. 6), expression (Art. 7), education (Art. 23) and the ban on discrimination (Art. 1).

Who counts as a "newcomer"?

Someone not born in NL who came to live here later — by choice (knowledge migrants) or necessity (refugees).

Define radicalisation and name a risk factor.

When someone's ideas/behaviour become extreme and clash with the norms of a democratic, rule-of-law state. Risk is higher among young people who don't feel at home, face discrimination, lack prospects, or feel strong "us vs. them".

What was pillarisation (verzuiling)?

Organising society around faith or political persuasion — separate schools, TV channels and newspapers for Catholics, Protestants and socialists.

Give one upside and one downside of individualisation.

Upside: more individual freedom and personal development. Downside: looser ties → loneliness, and a stronger focus on self.

Define globalisation and give an enrichment vs. a threat example.

People worldwide becoming more connected. Enrichment: new food/apps (shawarma, TikTok). Threat: loss of unique features, e.g. English replacing Dutch at universities → rise of nationalism.

Define emancipation and give one example.

People actively working for equal rights and treatment. Example: women's right to vote (1919) or same-sex marriage (2001).

What does LGBTQIA+ stand for, and name one recent success.

Lesbian, gay, bisexual, trans, queer/questioning, intersex, asexual and more. Successes: gender removed from the ID card, more gender-neutral toilets.

What does the SCP survey say about how the Netherlands is heading?

People are satisfied with their own lives but worried about co-existence — diminishing solidarity, less respect, growing division and polarisation.
